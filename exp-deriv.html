Pick a base b and move along \( y = b^x \). At each point, compare the slope with the height. Something surprising happens with their ratio. Try several bases.

  1. With b fixed, move x. What happens to slope ÷ height?
  2. Which base makes the ratio exactly 1? (Topic 0.3 might remind you.)
What you found

For each base, slope ÷ height is the same number everywhere on the curve. So the derivative of \( b^x \) is a constant times \( b^x \). For \( b = e \) that constant is exactly 1.

The rules
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\,e^x = e^x \qquad\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\,b^x = b^x \ln b \]

The constant \( \displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{b^h - 1}{h} \) equals \( \ln b \). You estimated the \( b = e \) case (it’s 1) in Topic 1.2. Topic 3.7 proves the general case with the Chain Rule.

Power vs. exponential

\( x^3 \) is a power (variable base, constant exponent), so use the power rule: \( 3x^2 \). \( 3^x \) is an exponential (constant base, variable exponent): \( 3^x \ln 3 \). The power rule does not apply to \( 3^x \), and \( \frac{d}{dx}3^x \ne x\cdot3^{x-1} \).

Also watch for constants in disguise: \( e^2 \), \( e^\pi \) and \( 5^3 \) are just numbers, with derivative 0. And \( e^{x+2} = e^2 \cdot e^x \) is a constant times \( e^x \).

Why it matters

“Rate of change proportional to amount” (\( y' = ky \)) describes population growth, radioactive decay, compound interest and cooling. Exponentials are the functions with that property, which is why they show up everywhere.
