At the marked point, f is…

f is differentiable at a if \( f'(a) \) exists, meaning the difference-quotient limit is a finite number. It’s differentiable on an open interval if that holds at every point in it.

The limit is two-sided, so it helps to look at each side separately. The one-sided derivatives are

\[ f'_-(a) = \lim_{h\to0^-}\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}, \qquad f'_+(a) = \lim_{h\to0^+}\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}. \]

\( f'(a) \) exists exactly when both are finite and equal.

Theorem: differentiable ⟹ continuous

If \( f'(a) \) exists, then f is continuous at a. The converse is false: \( |x| \) is continuous at 0 but not differentiable there.

Smoothing a seam

In Topic 1.7 you made the pieces of a function meet (continuity). Differentiability also needs them to meet at the same slope, so there’s no corner. That gives two conditions, so you need two unknowns.

\( f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2, & x \le 1 \\ mx + b, & x \gt 1 \end{cases} \). Find the m and b that make f differentiable at 1.

Algebra: continuity at 1 needs \( 1^2 = m + b \). Matching slopes needs the parabola’s slope at 1 (which is 2) to equal m. So \( m = 2 \), then \( b = -1 \). Always solve the slope equation first, since it usually involves only one unknown.
