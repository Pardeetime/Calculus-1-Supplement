To find the area of a region trapped between two curves, slice it into thin strips. Each strip is a skinny rectangle whose height is (top curve) − (bottom curve). Slide the red strip across each region. In scenario B, watch what happens to “top” and “bottom” partway across.

Area between curves

If \( f(x) \ge g(x) \) on \( [a, b] \), the area between them is

\[ A = \int_a^b \big[f(x) - g(x)\big]\,dx = \int_a^b (\text{top} - \text{bottom})\,dx. \]

Subtracting heights handles regions below the x-axis automatically, and the answer is always positive.

  1. Sketch both curves.
  2. Find where they intersect. Those x-values are often the limits.
  3. Decide which curve is on top. Test a point between the intersections.
  4. Integrate top − bottom. If the curves cross inside the interval, split the integral there so each piece uses the correct top curve.
When curves cross

\( \int_0^{\pi}(\sin x - \cos x)\,dx = 2 \), but the true area between sine and cosine on \( [0, \pi] \) is \( 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83 \). Past the crossing at \( \frac{\pi}{4} \), “sin − cos” is negative and cancels part of the area. Area needs \( \int |f - g|\,dx \), which in practice means splitting at each crossing.

Horizontal strips: integrate in y

Some regions are easier with horizontal strips, especially when the curves are written as \( x = \ldots \) or when “top” changes partway across. Then

\[ A = \int_c^d (\text{right} - \text{left})\,dy. \]

Exact answers preferred, like 9/2, e − 3/2, or 2sqrt(2) − 2.
