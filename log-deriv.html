\( \ln x \) is the mirror image of \( e^x \) across \( y = x \) (Topic 0.4). Reflecting a line across \( y = x \) swaps rise and run, which flips its slope upside down. Move the point on \( e^x \) and compare the two slopes.

What you see

At \( (a, e^a) \) the slope of \( e^x \) is \( e^a \). The mirror point on \( \ln x \) is \( (e^a, a) \), and its slope is \( \dfrac{1}{e^a} \). Call that point’s x-coordinate \( x = e^a \): the slope of \( \ln x \) there is simply \( \dfrac1x \).

Simplify with log laws first

\( \ln\!\left(\dfrac{x^3\sqrt{x+1}}{x^2+5}\right) = 3\ln x + \tfrac12\ln(x+1) - \ln(x^2+5) \). Three easy derivatives instead of one quotient-product-chain mess.

Logarithmic differentiation

For functions like \( x^x \), where the variable is in both the base and the exponent, neither the power rule nor the exponential rule applies. The fix: take ln of both sides, use log laws to bring the exponent down, then differentiate implicitly.

Common mistakes

\( \frac{d}{dx}x^x \ne x\cdot x^{x-1} \) (that’s the power rule, which needs a constant exponent) and \( \ne x^x \ln x \) (that’s the exponential rule, which needs a constant base). Also, \( \frac{d}{dx}\ln(x^2+1) \ne \dfrac{1}{x^2+1} \). Don’t forget the inside derivative.
