The Chain Rule says \( \frac{d}{dx}F(g(x)) = F'(g(x))\,g'(x) \). So an integrand shaped like “some function of g(x), times g′(x)” is a Chain Rule result, and we can run it backwards. The whole skill is choosing the right inside function, called u. Pick a u for each integral and see what happens.

What makes a good u

Choose u so that its derivative, du, is also sitting in the integrand, possibly off by a constant factor. Then every x disappears and you’re left with a basic integral in u. Good choices are usually the inside of a power, root, exponential or trig function, or a denominator.

Substitution Rule
\[ \int f\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)\,dx = \int f(u)\,du \qquad\text{where } u = g(x),\ du = g'(x)\,dx. \]
  1. Choose u, and compute \( du = u'\,dx \).
  2. Rewrite the entire integral in terms of u, with no x left over. Adjust constants if needed.
  3. Integrate in u.
  4. Substitute back \( u = g(x) \). (For definite integrals, see below.)

Definite integrals: change the limits

When you switch to u, switch the limits to u-values too: \( x = a \Rightarrow u = g(a) \). Then you never need to substitute back.

Common mistakes

Leaving some x’s in the integral after substituting. Forgetting to convert dx into du. Only a constant factor can be fixed by adjusting, never a leftover x. Keeping the old x-limits after switching to u. And forgetting to substitute back in indefinite problems.
