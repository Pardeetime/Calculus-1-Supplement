What is the area under \( y = x^2 \) from 0 to 2? No formula from geometry covers a curved region. But rectangles are easy. Cover the region with rectangles, add up their areas, and use more and more of them. What number do the sums close in on?

What you see

Left sums are too small here and right sums are too big, because the curve rises. Midpoints do much better. As n grows, all three close in on the same number, \( 2.6\overline{6} = \tfrac83 \). That limiting value is the area.

Split \( [a, b] \) into n equal pieces of width

\[ \Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n}, \qquad x_i = a + i\,\Delta x \quad (i = 0, 1, \ldots, n). \]

On each piece, build a rectangle whose height is f at some sample point, then add them up:

\[ \text{Area} \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x \]
SumSample point \( x_i^* \) on the i-th pieceFor an increasing f
Left \( L_n \)Left end, \( x_{i-1} \)Underestimate
Right \( R_n \)Right end, \( x_i \)Overestimate
Midpoint \( M_n \)Middle, \( \tfrac{x_{i-1} + x_i}{2} \)Usually the most accurate

For a decreasing function the left and right roles swap. The exact area is the limit as \( n \to \infty \), which is the idea behind the definite integral (Topic 5.2).

The distance problem

If velocity were constant, distance = velocity × time, the area of a rectangle on a velocity-time graph. When velocity changes, split time into short intervals, treat the velocity as roughly constant on each, and add. Distance traveled is the area under the velocity curve. Units check: (ft/s) × (s) = ft.

The big connection

Area under a rate gives a total amount: velocity gives distance, flow rate gives volume, and a birth rate gives a number of births. This one idea is behind every application of integrals.

Work these by hand, maybe with a calculator for the arithmetic. Exact answers or decimals are both fine.
