Every point on a smooth curve has its own slope. Drag the point along f (top). Its tangent slope is plotted as a height on the bottom graph. Sweep across the whole curve and the slopes trace out a brand-new function.

f(x)

slope of f at x

What you built

The slope graph is a function in its own right: the derivative function \( f' \). It takes an input x and returns the slope of f at x.

Definition
\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \]

It’s the same limit as in Topic 2.1, but with x left as a variable instead of a specific number a. The result is a formula that gives every slope at once. The domain of \( f' \) is every x where this limit exists.

Notation

NotationRead asNotes
\( f'(x) \), \( y' \)“f prime of x”Lagrange’s notation, compact
\( \dfrac{dy}{dx} \), \( \dfrac{df}{dx} \)“dee y dee x”Leibniz’s notation: a tiny change in y over a tiny change in x. It’s not a fraction you can cancel (at least not yet).
\( \dfrac{d}{dx}\big[x^2\big] \)“the derivative of \( x^2 \)”\( \frac{d}{dx} \) is an instruction: take the derivative of what follows.
\( \left.\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=3} \)“dy/dx at 3”Same as \( f'(3) \)

A pattern worth noticing

From the definition (you’ve done several of these already):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\,x = 1, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\,x^2 = 2x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\,x^3 = 3x^2 \]

Do you see the pattern? Unit 3 proves it for every power. Until then, the definition is your tool.

Higher derivatives

\( f' \) is a function, so it has its own derivative: the second derivative \( f'' = (f')' \), also written \( \dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} \). If \( s(t) \) is position, then \( s'(t) = v(t) \) is velocity and \( s''(t) = a(t) \) is acceleration, the rate at which velocity changes.

Use the limit definition, keeping x as a variable.
