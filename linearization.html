Close to the point of tangency, a curve and its tangent line are nearly the same (Topic 2.1). Lines are easy to evaluate and curves often aren’t. So use the line to estimate the curve. How close do you have to stay for the estimate to be good?

What you see

Near the base point the error is tiny, and it grows the farther you go. Whether the line lands above or below the curve depends on which way the curve bends.

Linearization of f at a
\[ L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a), \qquad f(x) \approx L(x) \text{ for } x \text{ near } a. \]

Choose a base point a that’s close to x and where f and \( f' \) are easy to compute exactly. For \( \sqrt{4.1} \), use \( a = 4 \).

Differentials

The same idea in different notation. If x changes by a small amount \( dx \), the true change in y is \( \Delta y \), and the tangent line’s prediction is the differential

\[ dy = f'(x)\,dx \approx \Delta y. \]

Differentials are great for error estimates. If a measurement is off by \( dx \), a computed result is off by about \( dy \).

Over or under?

If f is concave up (bending up, \( f'' \gt 0 \)), the tangent line lies below the curve, so L underestimates. If it’s concave down, L overestimates. \( \sqrt x \) is concave down, so \( L \) gave a slightly high estimate for \( \sqrt{4.1} \).

No calculator needed for the setups. Exact fractions or decimals are both fine for estimates.
