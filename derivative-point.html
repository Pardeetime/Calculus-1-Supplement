Local linearity

Zoom in far enough on a smooth curve and it becomes indistinguishable from a straight line, the tangent line. The slope of that line is the derivative at that point. It’s the one number that describes how steep the curve is right there.

Definition

The derivative of f at a is

\[ f'(a) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}, \]

provided this limit exists. An equivalent form, with \( x = a + h \), is \( \displaystyle f'(a) = \lim_{x\to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \).

You’ve already computed these in Units 0 and 1 without the name: the stone’s velocity, the slope of \( x^2 \) at 3, the \( (2+h)^2 \) limit. The definition is the secant slope from Topic 1.1 with a limit in front.

One number, two meanings:

  • Geometry: \( f'(a) \) is the slope of the tangent line at \( (a, f(a)) \). The tangent line is \( y = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) \).
  • Rates: \( f'(a) \) is the instantaneous rate of change of f at a, in output units per input unit.

Computing from the definition

The routine is always the same: build the difference quotient, simplify until the h in the denominator cancels, then let \( h \to 0 \). It’s Topic 1.4’s 0/0 toolkit applied to one particular fraction.

What a derivative means in context

Say \( C(q) \) is the cost in dollars of producing q bicycles, and \( C'(100) = 85 \). The units are dollars per bicycle, and it means:

When 100 bicycles have been made, the cost is increasing at $85 per additional bicycle. Making the 101st costs about $85.

A good interpretation says what is changing, how fast, in what units, and at what moment.

Common mistake

\( f'(a) \) is not \( f(a) \). “\( C'(100) = 85 \)” doesn’t mean 100 bicycles cost $85. It’s the rate at which the cost is climbing at that point.

Use the limit definition. (Unit 3 has shortcuts, but they’re built on this.)
