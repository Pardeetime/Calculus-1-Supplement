The circle \( x^2 + y^2 = 25 \) fails the vertical line test, so there’s no single formula \( y = f(x) \) for it. The curve on the right, the folium of Descartes, \( x^3 + y^3 = 6xy \), is even worse. But every point still has a tangent line. Drag the point around each curve. The slope formulas shown come from this lesson’s technique, and they work without ever solving for y.

The idea: near any point, the curve is the graph of some function \( y(x) \), even if we can’t write it down. So treat y as a function of x and differentiate both sides of the equation with respect to x.

The one new move

Whenever you differentiate something containing y, the Chain Rule attaches a factor of \( \frac{dy}{dx} \) (also written \( y' \)):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\,y^2 = 2y\,\frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\sin y = \cos y\,\frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}(xy) = y + x\,\frac{dy}{dx}. \]

The last one also needs the product rule, because both x and y are variables.

Steps: (1) Differentiate both sides. (2) Move every \( y' \) term to one side and everything else to the other. (3) Factor out \( y' \). (4) Divide.

Common mistakes

Forgetting the \( y' \) on y-terms (\( \frac{d}{dx}y^3 \ne 3y^2 \)). Differentiating a constant on the right side as anything but 0. And forgetting the product rule on terms like \( x^2 y \).

When the answer is a formula for \( \frac{dy}{dx} \), write it using x and y. Any equivalent form is accepted.
