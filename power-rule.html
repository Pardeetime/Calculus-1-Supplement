In Unit 2 you found \( \frac{d}{dx}x^2 = 2x \) by grinding through a limit. Here’s a picture of why. A square with side x has area \( A = x^2 \). Grow the side by a little bit, dx, and look at where the new area comes from.

Shrink dx. The two gold strips stay important, and the tiny red corner becomes negligible.

The pattern

New area ≈ two strips of size \( x \cdot dx \), so the area grows at a rate of \( 2x \). For a cube \( V = x^3 \), growing the side adds three slabs of size \( x^2\,dx \), so the rate is \( 3x^2 \). The exponent counts how many faces grow, and each face is one dimension smaller.

The Power Rule
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\,x^n = n\,x^{n-1} \qquad\text{for every real number } n. \]

Bring the exponent down in front, then subtract one from it.

For a positive whole number n, it comes straight from the definition: expanding \( (x+h)^n = x^n + n x^{n-1}h + (\text{terms with } h^2) \), the difference quotient is \( n x^{n-1} + (\text{terms with } h) \), which approaches \( n x^{n-1} \). Unit 3 later proves it for every real n.

Rewrite before you differentiate

The power rule needs the form \( x^n \). Roots and fractions have to be rewritten first:

Common mistakes

\( \frac{d}{dx}\dfrac{1}{x^2} \ne \dfrac{1}{2x} \). You can’t differentiate a denominator separately. Rewrite it as \( x^{-2} \) first. Also, the power rule moves exponents down, so \( x^{-2} \) becomes \( x^{-3} \), not \( x^{-1} \).
