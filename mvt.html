The gold secant line joins the ends of the curve on \( [a, b] \), and its slope is the average rate of change. Slide the point c along the curve until its red tangent line is parallel to the secant. Is there always such a point? Can there be more than one?

The idea

If a smooth curve starts at one height and ends at another, then somewhere in between, its instantaneous slope equals its average slope. If you drove 150 miles in 2 hours (averaging 75 mph), at some instant your speedometer read exactly 75.

Rolle’s Theorem

If f is continuous on \( [a, b] \), differentiable on \( (a, b) \), and \( f(a) = f(b) \), then \( f'(c) = 0 \) for some c in \( (a, b) \). What goes up and comes back down must level off somewhere.

Mean Value Theorem

If f is continuous on \( [a, b] \) and differentiable on \( (a, b) \), then for some c in \( (a, b) \):

\[ f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}. \]

It’s Rolle’s Theorem, tilted.

The hypotheses matter

\( f(x) = |x| \) on \( [-1, 1] \): the average slope is 0, but \( f' \) is only ever ±1. The MVT fails because of the corner, since f isn’t differentiable at 0. A jump breaks it too. Always check both conditions before using the theorem.

Why the MVT matters: equal derivatives

  • If \( f'(x) = 0 \) everywhere on an interval, then f is constant there. (Any two points have an average slope of \( f'(c) = 0 \).)
  • If \( f'(x) = g'(x) \) everywhere, then \( f(x) = g(x) + C \): the functions differ by a constant.

That second fact is why every antiderivative gets a “+ C” (Topic 4.8).
