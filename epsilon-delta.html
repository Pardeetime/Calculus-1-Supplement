We claim \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to2} x^2 = 4 \). A skeptic doesn’t believe it and issues a challenge: “Keep the output within ε of 4.” Your job is to answer with a δ: a distance so that every x within δ of 2 has an output inside the gold band.

Wherever the curve inside your purple strip leaves the gold band, it turns red. Make it all green, then lock it in. The challenges get harder.

What the game shows

No matter how small an ε the skeptic picks, you can always answer with some δ. That guarantee, for every ε, is exactly what it means for the limit to be 4.

Definition

\( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L \) means: for every \( \varepsilon \gt 0 \) there is a \( \delta \gt 0 \) such that

\[ 0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(x) - L| \lt \varepsilon. \]

Read it piece by piece:

  • \( |f(x) - L| \lt \varepsilon \): “the output is within ε of L” (the gold band).
  • \( |x - a| \lt \delta \): “the input is within δ of a” (the purple strip).
  • \( 0 \lt |x - a| \): “but x isn’t a itself,” because limits never look at a.
  • “For every ε there is a δ”: the skeptic moves first, and your δ is allowed to depend on their ε.

Linear functions: δ = ε / |slope|

For a line with slope m, moving the input by δ moves the output by exactly \( |m|\delta \). To keep the output change under ε, choose \( \delta = \varepsilon/|m| \). A steeper line needs a narrower strip.

Curves: find the largest δ from both sides

For a curve, the two sides of a usually allow different distances. Find each one, then take the smaller, because δ has to work on both sides at once.

Common mistakes

Picking the larger side distance: then on the tighter side some x escapes the band. And writing δ in terms of x: δ may depend on ε (and a), but never on x.
