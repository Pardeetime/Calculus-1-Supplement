A point travels around the unit circle at a steady speed of 1 (in radians per second, θ = t). Its height is \( \sin t \) and its horizontal position is \( \cos t \). The arrow is its velocity, always tangent to the circle and always of length 1. How fast is the height changing? How fast is the horizontal position changing?

The velocity arrow is the radius rotated 90°. Rotating \( (\cos t, \sin t) \) by 90° gives \( (-\sin t, \cos t) \).

What the arrow shows

The vertical part of the velocity is how fast the height \( \sin t \) changes, and it equals \( \cos t \). The horizontal part is how fast \( \cos t \) changes, and it equals \( -\sin t \). So \( (\sin t)' = \cos t \) and \( (\cos t)' = -\sin t \).

Pattern: the right column is the “co-” version of the left column, with co-swaps throughout and a minus sign. Every co-function’s derivative is negative.

The four-step cycle

\( \sin \to \cos \to -\sin \to -\cos \to \sin \). Differentiating sine four times brings you back to sine. To find the 27th derivative, use 27 = 4·6 + 3, so it’s the same as the 3rd.

Radians only

Every formula here assumes x is in radians, because they all rest on \( \frac{\sin h}{h} \to 1 \) (Topic 1.5).
