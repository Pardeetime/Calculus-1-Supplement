Start at 0 and sweep to the right, collecting the area under f as you go. The running total is a new function, \( A(x) = \int_0^x f(t)\,dt \), graphed below. Drag x and compare two numbers: the slope of A, and the height of f.

f(t), shading the area from 0 to x

A(x) = accumulated area so far

Where f is negative, the area collected is negative, so A goes down. Where f crosses zero, A levels off.

What you just saw

The slope of A equals the height of f, everywhere. Pushing x forward by a sliver h adds a thin strip of area, about \( f(x)\cdot h \), so A grows at rate \( f(x) \). Accumulating is the reverse of differentiating. That link between areas and slopes is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

FTC, Part 1

If f is continuous, then \( A(x) = \displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt \) is differentiable and

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x). \]

Every continuous function has an antiderivative, and here’s one.

FTC, Part 2 (the evaluation theorem)

If F is any antiderivative of f on \( [a, b] \), then

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) = \Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b. \]

The limit of a Riemann sum reduces to plugging two numbers into an antiderivative. You can use any antiderivative, because the +C cancels.

Part 1 with a chain

If the upper limit is a function \( g(x) \), the Chain Rule applies: \( \dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_a^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt = f\big(g(x)\big)\cdot g'(x) \). If x is the lower limit, flip the limits first, which adds a minus sign.

Common mistakes

Forgetting to subtract \( F(a) \), especially when it isn’t zero. Using a “+C” in a definite integral, which isn’t wrong but always cancels. Writing \( f(b) - f(a) \) instead of \( F(b) - F(a) \): evaluate the antiderivative, not the integrand. And in Part 1, forgetting the chain factor \( g'(x) \).
