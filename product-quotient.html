A rectangle has width \( f \) and height \( g \), and both are changing. Its area is the product \( f g \). When f grows by \( \Delta f \) and g grows by \( \Delta g \), where does the new area come from?

Here \( \Delta f = 1.5\,\text{(growth)} \) and \( \Delta g = \text{(growth)} \). Shrink the growth and watch the corner vanish.

What the picture says

The new area is two strips: g times the change in f, plus f times the change in g. The corner \( \Delta f\,\Delta g \) is tiny compared with both. So \( (fg)' = f'g + fg' \). It is not \( f'g' \), which would be just the corner.

Product Rule
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\big[f(x)\,g(x)\big] = f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x) \]

In words: “derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times the derivative of the second.”

Quotient Rule
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{f'(x)\,g(x) - f(x)\,g'(x)}{\big[g(x)\big]^2} \]

Memory aid: “low d-high minus high d-low, over the square of what’s below.” The order matters because of the minus sign.

Choose the easiest path

You don’t always need these rules. \( \dfrac{x^3 + 1}{x} \) splits into \( x^2 + x^{-1} \), and \( 5x^3 \) is a constant multiple, not a product. Use the product rule when both factors contain x, and the quotient rule when the denominator is more than a single term.
