Two walkers approach \( x = 2 \), one from the left and one from the right. Move them closer and compare the heights they report. Do the two sides always agree? And do they always approach a number?

One-sided limits

\[ \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = L \quad(\text{from the left: } x \lt a) \qquad \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = L \quad(\text{from the right: } x \gt a) \]

The little minus or plus sign tells you which side to approach from. It is not a negative number.

The key theorem

\( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L \) if and only if both one-sided limits exist and both equal L. If the sides disagree, the two-sided limit does not exist.

Infinite limits

If \( f(x) \) grows without bound as \( x \to a \), we write \( \lim_{x\to a} f(x) = \infty \). The outputs aren’t approaching any number, so strictly speaking the limit doesn’t exist. But \( \infty \) tells you how it fails, and that’s more useful than just “DNE.”

  • Both sides go to \( \infty \) (or both to \( -\infty \)): write \( \infty \) (or \( -\infty \)).
  • One side goes to \( \infty \) and the other to \( -\infty \): the two-sided limit is DNE, so describe each side separately.

The line \( x = a \) is a vertical asymptote if at least one one-sided limit is \( \pm\infty \).

Sign analysis: nonzero ÷ 0

If direct substitution gives a nonzero number over 0, the limit is infinite, or DNE. To decide which, find the sign of the numerator and the sign of the tiny denominator on each side.

Two different situations

\( \dfrac{\text{nonzero}}{0} \) means a vertical asymptote: infinite or DNE. \( \dfrac{0}{0} \) means nothing yet. It could be a hole, an asymptote or anything else, so you need algebra (Topic 1.4). Don’t mix them up.

Absolute values

Split \( |x - a| \) into pieces: it equals \( x - a \) when \( x \gt a \) and \( -(x - a) \) when \( x \lt a \). One-sided limits are the natural tool because each side uses one piece.

Answer with a number, inf, -inf or DNE.
