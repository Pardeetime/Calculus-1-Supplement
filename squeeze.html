Here’s \( f(x) = x^2 \sin\!\left(\dfrac1x\right) \). Near 0, \( \sin(1/x) \) oscillates infinitely fast, so the algebra from the last lesson can’t help. But look at the two gold curves. Zoom in toward the origin: where is \( f \) forced to go?

At every zoom level, the wiggly curve stays between \( -x^2 \) and \( x^2 \). Both bounds are pinched to 0 at the origin, and so is everything between them.

Squeeze Theorem

If \( g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x) \) for all x near a (except possibly at a), and

\[ \lim_{x\to a} g(x) = \lim_{x\to a} h(x) = L, \quad\text{then}\quad \lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L. \]

Where the bounds come from: nearly always from \( -1 \le \sin(\text{anything}) \le 1 \) (or cosine). Multiply through by a factor that’s never negative, like \( x^2 \) or \( |x| \), so the inequalities don’t flip.

Both bounds must meet

From \( -1 \le \sin(1/x) \le 1 \) alone you get nothing: the bounds −1 and 1 don’t squeeze to one number. And \( \lim \sin(1/x) \) really doesn’t exist. The squeeze only works when both bounds approach the same limit.

The famous one: \( \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \to 1 \)

You estimated this in Topic 1.2. Now you can prove it. Compare three areas inside the unit circle, for a small angle θ in radians:

The picture shows small triangle ≤ sector ≤ big triangle:

\[ \tfrac12\sin\theta \;\le\; \tfrac12\theta \;\le\; \tfrac12\tan\theta. \]

Multiply by \( \tfrac{2}{\sin\theta} \) (positive for small θ) and flip each fraction, which reverses the inequalities:

\[ \cos\theta \;\le\; \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \;\le\; 1. \]

As \( \theta \to 0 \), \( \cos\theta \to 1 \). Both bounds meet at 1, so by the Squeeze Theorem, \( \displaystyle \lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1 \).

Why calculus cares

This single limit is why \( \frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x \) (Unit 3). It’s also why radians are required: the sector area \( \tfrac12\theta \) is only that simple in radians.

Using it: match the angle to the denominator. For \( \dfrac{\sin 4x}{x} \), write \( 4 \cdot \dfrac{\sin 4x}{4x} \). The fraction has the form \( \dfrac{\sin\square}{\square} \) with \( \square \to 0 \), so it approaches 1, and the limit is \( 4 \).
