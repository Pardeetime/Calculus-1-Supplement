The big idea

A limit describes where the outputs are heading as x approaches a. It ignores what happens at a. The function might be undefined there, or defined as something else entirely, and the limit is the same either way.

We write

\[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \]

and say “the limit of \( f(x) \), as x approaches a, equals L” if we can make \( f(x) \) as close to L as we like by taking x close enough to a, from either side, but not equal to a. Topic 1.6 turns “close enough” into a precise definition.

There are three ways to find a limit:

  • Graphically: follow the curve toward \( x = a \) from both sides and see what height it approaches.
  • Numerically: make a table with x-values closer and closer to a on both sides.
  • Algebraically: use the limit laws and simplify (Topic 1.4). This is the only method that proves the answer.

Three ways a limit can fail to exist

Jump

The left and right sides approach different heights.

Blow-up

The outputs grow without bound, so they don’t approach any number.

Oscillation

The outputs keep bouncing between values and never settle.

Lesson

The table only checked points where \( \pi/x \) is a multiple of \( \pi \), which are exactly the points where sine happens to be 0. In between, the graph swings all the way between −1 and 1, faster and faster. The limit does not exist. Tables suggest answers, and algebra and theorems prove them.
