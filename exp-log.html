Every exponential graph \( y = b^x \) passes through the point \( (0, 1) \). The red line is the tangent line there, the line that just grazes the curve. Its steepness depends on the base b.

Your mission: find the base whose tangent line at \( (0,1) \) has a slope of exactly 1, so that it lands on the dashed guide line \( y = x + 1 \).

Tip: after dragging, fine-tune with the arrow keys.

What you just found

The special base is \( e \approx 2.71828\ldots \). It’s the only base where the slope at \( (0,1) \) is exactly 1. It also turns out that every point on \( y = e^x \) has a slope equal to its own height. That’s why \( e \) is called the natural base.

Exponential functions

An exponential function has the form \( f(x) = b^x \) with a base \( b \gt 0 \), \( b \ne 1 \). The variable is in the exponent, and that’s what separates it from a power function like \( x^2 \).

  • If \( b \gt 1 \), the function shows growth. If \( 0 \lt b \lt 1 \), it shows decay.
  • The domain is all real numbers and the range is \( (0, \infty) \). The output is never 0 or negative.
Exponent lawExample
\( b^m \cdot b^n = b^{m+n} \)\( e^2 e^3 = e^5 \)
\( \dfrac{b^m}{b^n} = b^{m-n} \)\( \dfrac{e^x}{e^2} = e^{x-2} \)
\( (b^m)^n = b^{mn} \)\( (e^x)^3 = e^{3x} \)
\( b^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{b^n} \), \( b^0 = 1 \)\( e^{-x} = \dfrac{1}{e^x} \)

Where else e shows up: compound interest

Put $1 in an account paying 100% interest per year, compounded n times per year. After one year you have \( \left(1 + \frac1n\right)^n \) dollars. Does compounding more often make you rich?

Logarithms: a log is an exponent

\( \log_b x \) answers one question: “b to what power gives x?”

\[ \log_b x = y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b^y = x \]

So \( \log_2 8 = 3 \) because \( 2^3 = 8 \). The natural log \( \ln x \) means \( \log_e x \). In calculus, you’ll use \( \ln \) almost every time.

Because a log undoes an exponential, \( \ln(e^x) = x \) and \( e^{\ln x} = x \) for \( x \gt 0 \). The domain of \( \ln x \) is \( (0, \infty) \), which is exactly the range of \( e^x \). You’ll see why in the next lesson on inverse functions.

Things that are NOT log laws

\( \ln(x + y) \ne \ln x + \ln y \).   \( (\ln x)^2 \ne 2\ln x \).   \( \dfrac{\ln x}{\ln y} \ne \ln\!\left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right) \). Try any of them with numbers on a calculator.
