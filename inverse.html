An inverse function runs a function backwards: outputs go in and the original inputs come out. On a graph, that means swapping every point \( (a, b) \) to \( (b, a) \). Move the point below and watch where its swapped twin lands.

A function is one-to-one if different inputs always give different outputs. Only one-to-one functions have inverses. If two inputs share an output, then running backwards from that output gives two answers, and that isn’t a function.

Horizontal line test

A function is one-to-one exactly when every horizontal line hits its graph at most once. Reflecting across \( y = x \) turns horizontal lines into vertical ones, so this is the vertical line test applied to the mirror image.

If \( f \) is one-to-one, its inverse \( f^{-1} \) is defined by

\[ f^{-1}(b) = a \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(a) = b. \]

Each one cancels the other: \( f^{-1}(f(x)) = x \) and \( f(f^{-1}(x)) = x \). The domain of \( f^{-1} \) is the range of \( f \), and the other way around.

Notation trap

\( f^{-1}(x) \) does not mean \( \dfrac{1}{f(x)} \). The −1 here means “inverse function,” not “reciprocal.”

Finding an inverse algebraically

  1. Write \( y = f(x) \).
  2. Solve for \( x \) in terms of \( y \). This is the “run it backwards” step.
  3. Swap the letters so the inverse uses \( x \) as its input: \( f^{-1}(x) = \ldots \)
  4. Check the domain. It should match the range of \( f \).

Restricting the domain

Some useful functions aren’t one-to-one, but you can keep just one piece of them. \( x^2 \) fails the horizontal line test, but on \( [0, \infty) \) it passes, and its inverse is \( \sqrt{x} \). That’s why \( \sqrt{9} = 3 \) and not \( \pm 3 \).

Why calculus cares

\( \ln x \) is defined as the inverse of \( e^x \). The inverse trig functions (arcsin, arctan, …) come from restricting sine and tangent. In Unit 3 you’ll find their derivatives using exactly the mirror picture above.
