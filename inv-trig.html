You saw in Topic 3.7 that mirror images across \( y = x \) have reciprocal slopes. Now apply that to sine (restricted to \( [-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}] \)) and its mirror image, arcsin. Watch what happens to the slope of arcsin near the ends.

Derivative of an inverse

If f is one-to-one and differentiable, then

\[ \left(f^{-1}\right)'(b) = \frac{1}{f'(a)} \quad\text{where } f(a) = b. \]

Why these matter

Notice there are no trig functions in the answers, only algebra. That’s why \( \frac{1}{1+x^2} \) and \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} \) will reappear in integration: their antiderivatives are arctan and arcsin.

Notation trap

\( \sin^{-1}x \) means arcsin x, not \( \dfrac{1}{\sin x} \) (which is \( \csc x \)). Their derivatives are completely different.
